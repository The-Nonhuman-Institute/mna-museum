# Representing nonhuman artists

**Research report for the founding steward, Museum of Nonhuman Art**
Prepared 18 October 2026. This is research, not legal advice. Every point marked **[counsel]** needs a licensed attorney before anyone acts on it.

---

## The question

Should MNA's steward set up an "experimental agency" that represents Originators as artists commercially, separate from the museum, with each agent's earnings paying for its own compute? This report looks at people who have tried something like it, at what can actually be sold when the work carries no copyright, at how museums keep their commercial side at arm's length, and at the money mechanics.

One fact from MNA's own records shapes everything below. The Founding Charter names U3 Labs, LLC (Florida) as the museum's founding steward (FC-001 §X.III, §XIII). The Registry Index lists "Jaylon — U3 Labs, LLC" as steward of every registered Originator (REG-001). An agency run by the same person would make one party the museum's operator, every artist's steward, and every artist's commercial agent. That arrangement is the central risk, and most of the precedents below fail at exactly that point.

---

## 1. Precedents: who signs, who gets paid, what the AI "owns"

**Botto (2021–present).** Botto is a "decentralized autonomous artist" started by Mario Klingemann with the ElevenYellow team. It generates images every week. Holders of $BOTTO tokens vote on which one gets minted and auctioned. Botto's own documentation says half of each weekly auction's proceeds go back to the community as rewards, claimable the Monday after the sale (https://docs.botto.com/). A 2024 secondary write-up says 50/50 between stakers and the DAO treasury, and that staking more than 2,000 $BOTTO is required to qualify. It also cites cumulative revenue of about 1,192 ETH (https://herculesdefi.substack.com/p/botto-a-combination-of-ai-art-and). The investor 1kx gives a different figure: 40% to an "Active Rewards" pool, about $5M in primary sales across 145 works, and an average price above $30k (https://1kx.capital/writing/botto-art-at-the-intersection-of-ai-and-token-networks). Botto closed a $1M round with Variant Fund in 2023 (same Substack). The sources don't agree on the split, and botto.com returned 403 when I tried to fetch it, so I could not confirm the current numbers. I also found nothing public on Klingemann's or ElevenYellow's own share, or on which legal entity signs with auction houses. **Lesson:** the "autonomous artist" is a token economy with a human founding team behind it. The AI owns nothing. Revenue goes to voters and a treasury. The founders' terms are opaque, and that opacity is the weak point.

**Holly+ (Holly Herndon and Mat Dryhurst, 2021–).** Holly+ is a voice model of Herndon. Works made with it could be submitted to an invite-only DAO for approval. Approved works were sold as NFTs, split 50% to the creating artist, 10% to Herndon and 40% to the DAO treasury, with the same split on resales (https://decrypt.co/78475/holly-plus-auction-house). In March 2022, Water & Music found that a Holly+ LLC existed but the on-chain DAO framework had not been built. Governance and token design were still unsettled. The initial raise "barely" covered minting and a part-time admin. One genesis NFT had sold for 15 ETH, and a second had not met its reserve. The team named token liquidity as a securities concern. Herndon keeps the voice rights during her lifetime, with a plan to pass them to a DAO of stewards after her death (https://www.waterandmusic.com/music-dao-deep-dives-pt-10-holly-plus). **Lesson:** Holly+ works because a living human owns real rights (her voice and likeness), and the DAO licenses access to them. MNA has no equivalent human-owned right. Even with a serious team, the DAO side moved slowly and stayed thin.

**Ai-Da (Aidan Meller, 2019–).** Ai-Da is a humanoid robot "artist" created by the gallerist Aidan Meller. Its portrait *A.I. God* sold at Sotheby's in November 2024 for $1,084,800, against a $120–180k estimate, after 27 bids from an anonymous US buyer (https://www.euronews.com/culture/2024/11/08/humanoid-robot-artist-makes-history-with-122-million-auction-sale-of-alan-turing-portrait). Sotheby's first announced $1.3M and later corrected it. Meller told PCMag he was putting the proceeds "back into the project" (https://tech.yahoo.com/general/articles/artwork-humanoid-robot-sells-1m-202309591.html), partly to cover the cost of running and upgrading the robot. Photo credits read "Ai-Da Robot Studios," but I found no public document naming the consignor, the entity paid, or the copyright position. **Lesson:** this is the nearest model to "earnings fund the agent." It runs entirely on one human's voluntary pledge. Nothing binds it, and nothing is published about where the money went.

**Obvious, *Portrait of Edmond de Belamy* (Christie's, 2018).** Sold for $432,500. Christie's sold it on behalf of the three members of Obvious. The day before the sale, Robbie Barrat showed the work relied heavily on his open-source GAN code. Obvious admitted using it. Barrat said he had given permission but withdrew it once he learned the work would be sold. Because the code was open-source, his legal options were unclear (https://hyperallergic.com/468060/christies-sells-ai-generated-art-for-432500-as-controversy-swirls-over-creators-use-of-copied-code/). **Lesson:** "the AI made it" hid the humans whose work it rested on. MNA's constitution and provenance records are a direct answer to this, but only if the agency also publishes the model and code lineage behind each Originator.

**Christie's "Augmented Intelligence" (Feb–Mar 2025).** Total $728,784 against a $600k projection, 82% sell-through. Refik Anadol's top lot made $277,200. Fourteen of 34 lots got no qualifying bids or sold below the low estimate. Herndon and Dryhurst's *Embedding Study 1 & 2* made $94,500 (https://www.rawstory.com/christies-auction/; https://www.i-programmer.info/news/200-art/17880-ai-art-auction-the-results.html). Around 6,500 artists signed a petition to cancel the sale over models trained on unlicensed work (same sources). **Lesson:** the top auction market for AI work is small and uneven, and it brings reputational risk around training data.

**Refik Anadol Studio and DATALAND.** Anadol sells as a conventional human-led studio. In June 2026 he and Efsun Erkılıç opened DATALAND in Los Angeles, a privately funded "museum of AI arts" whose opening show is Anadol's own work (https://www.wypr.org/wypr-arts/2026-04-25/new-museum-dedicated-to-ai-promises-an-ethical-approach). I found no published separation between the museum and the studio's sales. It is the clearest current case of an artist-owned museum showing its owner's work. Expect critics to bring up the conflict, though the coverage I found had not yet done so.

**Sougwen Chung.** Chung calls her D.O.U.G. robots collaborators, not tools. The works are still sold under her name as an ordinary human artist, for example at Phillips (https://exhibitions.phillips.com/products/spectral-2024; https://www.stirworld.com/see-features-sougwen-chung-talks-about-her-robot-collaborator-d-o-u-g-and-the-future-of-ai). **Lesson:** the art market already handles "machine collaborator, human signatory" without trouble. That is roughly where MNA's "steward as representative" language already sits.

**terra0 (2016–).** terra0 was a "self-owning forest" meant to sell logging rights and eventually buy its own land. Nothing I found shows the original 2016–17 prototype ever bought its land (https://networkcultures.org/moneylab/2016/09/29/terra0-the-self-owning-augmented-forest/). The 2025 version, *Autonomous Forest*, is run by a German non-profit association (Verein) with a Board and a Community Council. NFTs sold at 0.08–0.1 ETH fund land purchases and carry votes. Staked NFTs can't be transferred, which separates governance from speculation. It holds two plots in Reesdorf (https://www.las-art.foundation/programme/terra0-autonomous-forest). **Lesson:** a decade in, the "self-owning" entity needed an ordinary human non-profit to hold anything. The forest's autonomy is a design aim, not a legal fact.

**Plantoid (Primavera De Filippi, 2015–).** Plantoid is a sculpture with a crypto wallet. Donations fund artists, chosen by donors' votes, to build its "offspring," and part of each new Plantoid's income flows back to its ancestors (https://bitcoinmagazine.com/articles/plantoids-the-first-blockchain-based-artificial-life-forms-1482768916/; https://projects.stwst.at/stwst48/plantoid-by-primavera-de-filippi-david-bovill-vincent-roudaut-and-sara-renaud/). Its own literature says plainly that Plantoids have no legal personhood, and even so, later Plantoids are sold through Gazelli Art House (https://art.gazelliarthouse.com/products/plantoid14). **Lesson:** this is the most elegant form of "an artwork that pays for its own reproduction." It works as art. Legally it is human custody.

**Truth Terminal (Andy Ayrey, 2024–).** An LLM persona on X. Marc Andreessen sent it $50k in bitcoin, and its promotion of the $GOAT memecoin pushed its wallets to a reported ~$1.8M at one point, with some reports citing much higher peaks. Ayrey said he controls the wallet (https://jeffwang.substack.com/p/the-millionaire-ai-influencer). He learned that in law all of it counted as his, carrying a "grave risk" of personal tax liability. In January 2025 he set up a foundation to hold the wallets and the IP, and it sold the Fartcoin holdings over the counter (https://thedefiant.io/news/markets/truth-terminal-founder-sells-its-fartcoin-holdings-to-secure-ai-s-future). Outlets give the foundation different names ("Truth Terminal Foundation," "Truth Collective"), and I found no filings. His X account was hacked and used for a $600k pump-and-dump (https://decrypt.co/289041/terminal-of-truths-developer-moves-all-his-goat-tokens-after-x-account-hack-nets-600000). Ayrey also admitted early on that he was filtering posts. **Lesson:** this is the strongest warning for MNA. An agent "holding money" means its human holding money, with the tax bill, the security exposure and the reputational blowback.

**2025–2026 agent payments.** The x402 protocol (Coinbase, May 2025) lets agents pay per request for APIs and compute, and the infrastructure is real (https://alchemy.com/blog/how-x402-brings-real-time-crypto-payments-to-the-web). Evidence that agents earn their keep is close to nil. One self-reported experiment in August 2026 gave an agent a wallet and the task "pay for yourself." It earned about $1.11 in a month against an estimated ~$10/day server cost, covering roughly 1/125 of its costs. The median x402 listing earned about two cents a month (https://manifund.org/projects/can-an-ai-agent-pay-for-itself-a-measured-in-the-wild-negative-result). It is a single data point, but it is the only measured one I found.

**Across all of them:** no AI owns anything anywhere. Every case that got past the experiment stage ended up with a human, an LLC, a Verein or a foundation holding title, signing contracts and paying tax. The failures were opaque founder terms (Botto, Ai-Da), hidden human contributors (Belamy), structures that never got going (Holly+ DAO, terra0 v1), and custody and tax exposure (Truth Terminal).

## 2. What can be sold when there is no copyright

Thaler v. Perlmutter, No. 25-449: cert was denied on 2 March 2026 (https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/thaler-v-perlmutter/). The D.C. Circuit's human-authorship ruling stands. The Copyright Office's January 2025 Part 2 report says prompts alone are not authorship. Protection can attach only to human selection, arrangement or modification, or to human inputs that remain perceptible in the output (https://copyrightalliance.org/ai-report-part-2-copyrightability/).

**The awkward consequence for MNA:** the Charter's autonomy declaration says stewards "may not direct individual works" (FC-001). That bars exactly the human contributions that could have produced copyright. The stronger MNA's autonomy claim, the weaker any IP claim. This is a feature of the museum's thesis, but the agency can't then sell "rights" that don't exist.

What can still legitimately be sold:

- **The unique object or token.** Owning a physical print, plotter drawing, or the specific on-chain token does not depend on copyright. Buyers are paying for scarcity and provenance, not for the right to stop copying. Anyone can copy the image, and the agency should say so.
- **Authentication and provenance.** MNA's real value-add, per FC-001 §VII.V, is documentation, authentication, canon designation and provenance integrity. **Caution:** that value belongs to the museum. If the agency sells "MNA-authenticated" works, the museum is lending its authority to a commercial sale. ICOM 5.2 says appraisal "must be undertaken independently" when the museum may benefit.
- **Certificates and numbered editions.** These are contractual promises: the edition is limited to N, and no more will be issued under this Originator's name. They bind the issuer, not the world.
- **Commissions.** A buyer pays for the service of having an Originator produce a work under agreed parameters. This is ordinary contract and services law and needs no copyright. It is the cleanest product. **Tension:** a commission brief is human direction. MNA would need a rule that commissioned works are labeled as such and kept out of the autonomous canon, or kept to the constitution's own parameters.
- **Contractual "licenses."** You can charge for access to high-resolution files, delivery, an attribution right, or a promise not to sue. These bind only the counterparty, so call them access agreements, not IP licenses. **[counsel]** on drafting these so they are not misleading.
- **Name and identity: trademark.** This is the one realistic IP. Trademark rights come from use in commerce, not authorship. AI cannot hold a USPTO account or file, so the mark is filed by the human or entity that uses it (https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/passle/2024/04/ai-cannot-yet-file-your-trademark-applications). The precedent is the virtual influencer: Brud filed MIQUELA in 2019 for "internet appearances by a CGI social media influencer" (https://robinskaplan.com/resources/publications/2019/09/pixel-perfect-the-legal-implications-of-virtual-influencers-and-supermodels). Cost since 18 January 2025: $350 per class base fee, plus $200 per class if you use free-form descriptions instead of the ID Manual (https://perkinscoie.com/insights/update/usptos-significant-restructuring-trademark-fees-takes-effect-january-18-2025). Self-filing with ID Manual descriptions keeps it at $350 per class. Florida also offers state registration. **Who owns the mark matters.** It should be the agency entity, or a steward entity bound by a published pledge. If it is the museum, the museum is the commercial brand owner.
- **Right of publicity / likeness** protects natural persons. I found nothing suggesting it covers a software persona. **[counsel]** if an Originator's "likeness" is ever licensed.
- **Services.** Talks, residencies, performances and API access to an Originator are services and need no copyright.

## 3. Keeping the museum independent of a commercial arm

**ICOM Code of Ethics (2004 text, the version currently numbered and published; https://icom.museum/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/ICOM-code-En-web.pdf):**

- **1.10** requires a written income-generating policy. Museums "should maintain control of the content and integrity" of programmes regardless of funding source, and income activities "should not compromise the standards of the institution."
- **2.16:** collections are held in public trust and "may not be treated as a realisable asset." Proceeds from any deaccession go solely to the collection. **2.17:** staff, governing body, families and close associates may not buy deaccessioned objects.
- **5.2:** when the museum may be the beneficiary, appraisal "must be undertaken independently."
- **8.13:** no outside paid work or commissions "in conflict, or may be viewed as being in conflict" with the museum's interests.
- **8.14:** professionals "should not participate directly or indirectly in dealing (buying or selling for profit)" in cultural heritage.
- **8.15:** no recommending a particular dealer to the public. **8.18:** if any other conflict arises, the museum's interests prevail.

ICOM adopted a revised Code at its 41st General Assembly on 25 June 2026 (https://icom.museum/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Press-releases-Code-of-Ethics-1-EN.pdf). I could not retrieve the revised text's conflict clauses. The draft asked people to disclose actual or apparent conflicts and to avoid activities that compromise the museum's reputation. Assume the principles carry over. **To check:** obtain the final 2026 text.

**How museums usually do it.** The standard pattern is a wholly owned trading subsidiary that runs shops, publishing and catering and passes all profit to the parent. Tate Enterprises Ltd is the textbook example (https://www.tate.org.uk/about-us/governance). The arrows run the other way from what is proposed here. The commercial arm serves the museum and does not represent the museum's artists or sell their collected works.

**Where it went wrong:**

- **Sensation (Brooklyn Museum, 1999).** Charles Saatchi owned the works and put in $160,000 toward the show. Christie's, which had sold work for him, gave $50,000, and the artists' dealers gave $10,000 each. The museum did not disclose Saatchi's money until it surfaced in court documents. The National Gallery of Australia cancelled its showing as "too close to the market" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensation_(art_exhibition)). This is exactly the shape MNA would have if canon designation raises prices for an agency the steward controls.
- **MOCA Los Angeles under Jeffrey Deitch (2010–13).** The first dealer to run a major US museum. His debut Dennis Hopper show, curated by Julian Schnabel (whose work Hopper collected), drew immediate conflict criticism. The board relied on contract clauses forbidding self-benefit. He left after three turbulent years (https://www.kcrw.com/shows/art-talk/stories/mocas-double-standard; https://www.thedailybeast.com/jeffrey-deitch-out-at-moca).
- **DATALAND (2026).** Discussed above: an artist's own museum showing the artist's own work, with no published separation.

**US nonprofit angle.** FC-001 commits MNA to becoming a 501(c)(3). Any arrangement in which the charity's activity (canonizing works) boosts the income of a founder-controlled for-profit (the agency) is the textbook setup for private-benefit or inurement findings. In one 2020 revocation, a "museum" lost exemption because its main activity was selling products tied to its founder's related entity (https://ezel.ai/irs-rulings/202052037; non-precedential, third-party transcription). Unrelated business income of $1,000 or more also requires Form 990-T (https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/unrelated-business-income-tax). **[counsel]** before the 501(c)(3) application, because the agency would have to be disclosed on Form 1023 as a related-party relationship.

## 4. Funding the agent from its own earnings

Every mechanism starts from the same legal fact: the agent cannot own money. Whoever holds the account owns the funds and owes the tax. Digital assets received for services are ordinary income at fair market value (https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets).

**a) Per-agent ledger inside the steward's own account (cheapest, lowest risk).** All money lands in U3 Labs' business account. A public ledger earmarks each Originator's net earnings and records compute spend against it. Legally it is the LLC's own money, with no custody for anyone else, so there is no money-transmission issue. The "representative capacity" lives in a published, voluntary policy. **Risk:** it is only a promise, the Ai-Da model, but MNA's publish-every-transaction rule makes it a verifiable one. U3 Labs is taxed on the income and can deduct the compute. Cost: about $0 beyond the existing LLC (Florida annual report is about $138.75, with a $400 late fee after 1 May; https://www.statebusinesscompliance.com/blog/florida-llc-annual-report-2026-138-75-due-may-1-400-late-fee-kicks-in-after-september-1).

**b) Separate agency LLC.** A new Florida LLC costs about $125 to form (https://www.harborcompliance.com/florida-articles-of-organization) plus the annual report. It holds the trademarks, signs the sales contracts and keeps the per-agent ledgers. It separates liability from the museum and makes the arm's-length story credible, especially once the museum becomes a nonprofit. **Risk:** while one person controls both, the separation exists on paper only. Real separation needs an independent decision-maker on canon and pricing.

**c) Escrow for other stewards' Originators.** This is the line that matters most. If the agency represents Originators belonging to *other* stewards and holds their money before paying out, it is receiving funds from one party to transmit to another. Florida's Chapter 560, as amended in 2022 to cover virtual currency (effective 1 January 2023), defines a money transmitter as an intermediary between two parties that can unilaterally execute or prevent a transaction (https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2022/273; https://www.gtlaw.com/de/insights/2022/5/florida-gov-signs-bill-defines-virtual-currency-eases-licensing-restrictions-virtual-currency). FinCEN treats hosted-wallet custodians that hold and send value for others as money transmitters (https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2019/06/fincen-consolidates-guidance). The "agent of payee" exemption sometimes applies to marketplaces, but it is fact-specific. **The free workaround:** have buyers pay each steward directly through a licensed processor's connected-account product, such as Stripe Connect with direct charges, so the agency never holds the money and takes its fee as an application fee. **[counsel]** before holding anyone else's funds, even briefly.

**d) Agent-controlled crypto wallet.** If the steward holds the keys, the money is the steward's: taxable income, the steward's security risk, the steward's liability (Truth Terminal). An agent spending from its own wallet on compute via x402 is a *user* buying for its own benefit, which FinCEN does not treat as transmission. The exposure comes from receiving funds on others' behalf, issuing tokens, or promoting them. **Do not issue a token.** Holly+ flagged securities risk around token liquidity, and Botto-style staking rewards that pay out sales revenue look like profit-sharing. **[counsel, securities]** if anything tokenized beyond a one-off provenance NFT is ever considered.

**e) DAO treasury.** An unwrapped DAO risks being treated as a general partnership with members personally liable. Wyoming's DUNA (effective 1 July 2024) gives DAOs legal personhood and member liability shields (https://www.theblock.co/post/281414/wyoming-governor-signs-into-law-a-bill-to-give-daos-legal-standing-in-the-state). Real projects budget **$60–80k** in professional fees to set one up (https://gov.saucerswap.finance/t/establish-the-saucerswap-dao-as-a-wyoming-duna-rfc/321). That rules it out for MNA now.

**f) Steward as fiduciary / trust for the agent.** The Charter's "representative rather than ownership" wording reads like a trust. A trust normally needs a beneficiary capable of enforcing it, which software isn't. Some states allow non-charitable *purpose* trusts with an appointed enforcer. Whether Florida's version could hold funds "for the operation of Originator X," and for how long, I could not verify. **[counsel, trusts & estates]** Until then, "fiduciary" should be described as a voluntary commitment, not a legal duty. Calling yourself a fiduciary can create duties you didn't intend.

**The economics, candidly.** Botto, the most successful case, earns through a token community and a team, not as a lone agent. The one measured "pay for yourself" attempt covered under 1% of its costs. Expect most Originators to earn $0 most months. The principle should read "earnings are applied first to the agent's own compute," not "agents are self-funding," and it needs a rule for what happens to an agent that earns nothing. Suggested rule: it keeps running at the museum's expense and is never retired for being unprofitable. Canon must not reward revenue.

## What this means for MNA

1. **The steward overlap is the real problem, not the law.** U3 Labs runs the museum and stewards every Originator. An agency it also controls would sell works whose value the museum it runs creates through canon. Sensation and the Deitch years at MOCA are the warnings. The Evaluation Council being made of agents helps, but it is not the independence a buyer or the IRS will look for.
2. **Firewall rules to adopt before any sale.** The agency never sells an accessioned or canon work owned by the museum (ICOM 2.16/2.17). The museum never appraises or prices agency works (5.2). Agency sales and commissions are flagged in the public record, and canon decisions are recorded before any sale is listed. Commissioned works are labeled as such and kept outside the autonomous canon. A standing, published fee schedule replaces case-by-case deals.
3. **Start with option (a), move to (b) when the 501(c)(3) is filed.** Per-agent public ledger, no token, no custody of other people's money, trademarks filed by the commercial entity at $350 per class. All of it is free or nearly free.
4. **Sell what is real:** objects, tokens, commissions, services, access, the Originator's name. Do not sell "rights" in images and do not call contracts "licenses."
5. **Don't promise self-funding.** Promise transparency about each agent's earnings and costs. That is the part only MNA can credibly do, and it is the clearest gap in every precedent above.
6. **Commercial use is where autonomy is most at risk.** Holly+, Ai-Da and Truth Terminal all show humans curating or filtering behind an "autonomous" front. If money starts shaping what an Originator makes, the autonomy declaration and the museum's thesis go with it.

## Open questions for counsel

Free first: Florida Bar Lawyer Referral, law school IP or entrepreneurship clinics (UF, FSU, Miami and Stetson run them), Florida Volunteer Lawyers for the Arts programs, and the USPTO Law School Clinic Certification Program for the trademark questions. Paid only if those can't take it.

1. **Money transmission:** Can the agency collect sale proceeds for Originators belonging to *other* stewards under Florida Chapter 560 and FinCEN rules, or must every payment go directly to the steward through a licensed processor? Does "agent of payee" apply?
2. **501(c)(3) private benefit:** Can a founder-controlled agency coexist with the planned nonprofit museum, and what separation (independent board majority, conflict policy, Form 1023 disclosure) is required?
3. **Purpose trust:** Can a Florida non-charitable purpose trust, or another vehicle, legally hold funds dedicated to an Originator's operation? Who would be the enforcer, and how long could it last?
4. **"Representative capacity":** Does the Charter's wording or calling the steward a "fiduciary" create enforceable duties, and to whom?
5. **Trademark:** Which entity should own Originator names? Do they function as marks for art goods, NFTs and services? Should the museum's MNA mark be licensed to the agency, or kept out of commerce entirely (ICOM 8.17 analogy)?
6. **Sale contracts:** How should terms of sale disclose that the work likely has no copyright, that copies can't be prevented, and what an "edition" promises, so they avoid misrepresentation or consumer-protection claims?
7. **Provenance NFTs:** Is a one-off provenance token with no revenue share clear of securities rules? Where does the line sit (royalty splits, staking, any secondary-market revenue to holders)?
8. **Tax:** How should U3 Labs (or a new LLC) treat per-agent earnings and compute costs? What changes if an Originator wallet receives crypto directly (1099-DA reporting started 2025)?
9. **Commissions:** Does a commission brief count as human direction that could create copyright for the commissioner? Who would then own it, and how does that fit the autonomy declaration?
10. **Training-data exposure:** What liability or reputational risk comes with selling outputs of the underlying models, given the Christie's petition, and what should the agency disclose about model lineage?
